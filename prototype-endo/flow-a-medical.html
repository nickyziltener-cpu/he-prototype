Medical & surgical

6 options — all require a GP or gynaecologist conversation first.

Talk to a doctor before starting

All options in this section require medical assessment. We show you the evidence so you can have a better-informed conversation — not so you can self-prescribe.

Keyhole surgery to remove lesions
Strong evidence
The gold standard for both diagnosing and treating endometriosis. A surgeon makes small cuts in the abdomen and uses a tiny camera and instruments to find and remove lesions — this is the "keyhole" approach. Removing the tissue at the root (rather than burning the surface) is what guidelines recommend, and it has the clearest evidence for lasting pain relief. It's also the only way to get a definitive endometriosis diagnosis.
Hormone injections to pause oestrogen (Zoladex, Prostap)
Strong evidence (short-term)
Monthly injections (or implants) that temporarily switch off oestrogen production — putting the body into a reversible menopause-like state that starves endometriosis lesions of the hormone they need to grow. Highly effective for pain, but usually limited to 3–6 month courses due to side effects including hot flushes and bone density loss. Often prescribed alongside low-dose HRT to ease those effects.
Mirena coil (hormonal IUD)
Good evidence
Delivers progesterone directly to the uterus, reducing menstrual bleeding and endometriosis lesion activity. Multiple trials show significant pain reduction — and it offers long-term contraception at the same time. One of the most tolerable hormonal options for many women.
Combined oral contraceptive pill
Good evidence
Often the first-line medical treatment for endometriosis. Suppresses oestrogen-driven lesion growth and reduces menstrual bleeding and pain. Continuous use (skipping the pill-free week) is often more effective than cyclic use for pain management.
Progesterone-only therapy
Good evidence
Includes the mini-pill (e.g. Cerazette), Depo-Provera injection, and dienogest — a progesterone specifically licensed for endometriosis in some countries. Suppresses endometriosis tissue growth directly. Often preferred when oestrogen is not suitable. Effect on fertility varies by type — discuss with your gynaecologist.
Ibuprofen and naproxen (anti-inflammatories)
Limited evidence
Anti-inflammatory painkillers that reduce the compounds responsible for menstrual cramping. A Cochrane review found no trial evidence that ibuprofen or naproxen specifically reduces endometriosis pain. They do work for general period cramping, which is why they're widely used. Honest rating: effective for cramping, not yet proven for the deeper, chronic pain of endo itself.
