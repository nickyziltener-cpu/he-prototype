Mind & Body

6 options, ranked by strength of evidence for endometriosis.

Pelvic floor physiotherapy
Strong evidence
Specialist physiotherapy targeting the pelvic floor muscles. In endometriosis, chronic pain causes the pelvic floor to tense and lock tight — which amplifies pain on its own, even when the underlying condition isn't flaring. A 2024 systematic review confirmed significant pain reduction — with effect sizes comparable to hormonal treatments, and none of the side effects.

Pain psychology / CBT
Good evidence
Structured therapy that changes how the brain and body respond to chronic pain. Strong evidence for quality-of-life improvement in endometriosis — particularly for the spiral of fear and dread around pain, and the depression and anxiety that often accompany it.
TENS machine
Moderate evidence
A small device that sends gentle electrical pulses through the skin to block pain signals and trigger your body's natural painkillers (endorphins). A meta-analysis of 13 trials confirmed effectiveness for period pain. Endo-specific trials are limited, but the mechanism is the same and it's widely used alongside other treatments. Low-risk, home-use, no drug interactions.
Heat therapy
Moderate evidence
Heat relaxes uterine muscle and reduces prostaglandin-driven cramping. One RCT found continuous low-level heat was as effective as ibuprofen for menstrual pain. Highly accessible and can be combined with other approaches.
Yoga & gentle movement
Moderate evidence
Pelvic-focused yoga consistently shows pain and quality-of-life improvement in endometriosis. Combines gentle pelvic stretching with calming the body's stress response — two things that independently reduce pain.
Acupuncture
Mixed evidence
Some trials show meaningful pain reduction for endometriosis and period pain; others show no benefit over placebo acupuncture. Effect on inflammation markers is more consistent. Worth considering if other non-hormonal options haven't been enough.
