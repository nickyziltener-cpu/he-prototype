Supplements

5 options, ranked by strength of evidence for endometriosis.

N-acetyl cysteine (NAC)
Good evidence
An antioxidant that disrupts the growth of endometriosis tissue. One Italian trial (64 women) compared NAC to untreated controls — women taking it saw meaningful reduction in endometriosis cysts, and 21 of 47 with planned surgery chose to cancel it. Strong signal from a single well-designed trial. Larger studies are needed, but the evidence is compelling enough to warrant serious consideration. Low cost, widely available.

Vitamin D
Moderate evidence
Vitamin D deficiency is significantly more common in women with endometriosis than the general population. Several observational studies show an association between low vitamin D levels and greater disease severity and pain. Simple, low-risk and inexpensive to supplement.
Magnesium
Moderate evidence
Relaxes uterine muscle and reduces the pain-driving compounds responsible for cramping — the same mechanism as ibuprofen, but without the drug. Multiple trials show effectiveness for period pain, and it's widely used for endo-related pain with good tolerability.
Omega-3 fish oil
Moderate evidence
The active omega-3 compounds (EPA and DHA) reduce the inflammation that drives pelvic pain. A systematic review found meaningful reduction in period pain severity — a supplement form of the same anti-inflammatory effect as the omega-3 rich diet.
Curcumin (turmeric extract)
Limited evidence
Laboratory studies show curcumin inhibits endometrial cell growth and reduces inflammatory markers. Human trial evidence is early-stage and limited, but the anti-inflammatory profile and strong safety record make it worth considering as part of a broader strategy.
