N-acetyl cysteine (NAC)

An antioxidant supplement that in a head-to-head trial showed meaningful reductions in endometriosis cyst size — with 21 of 47 women cancelling planned surgery after 3 months of use.

What it is

N-acetyl cysteine is a naturally occurring compound the body uses to make glutathione — its main internal antioxidant. It reduces inflammation and appears to slow the growth of endometriosis cells. The working theory is that endometriosis creates an environment of oxidative stress that promotes lesion growth; NAC appears to disrupt this process.

It's sold over the counter as a supplement, is widely available, and costs around £10–20 per month. It's also used medically for liver protection and respiratory conditions.

How research suggests using it
1
Standard protocol from the trial
600mg NAC, three times daily, taken for 3 days on and 3 days off (cyclic dosing). This was the protocol used in the Porpora et al. trial.
2
Minimum trial period: 3 months
The study ran for 3 months before measuring outcomes. Symptom effects take time — expect to assess properly after 2–3 cycles.
3
Discuss with a doctor first
Particularly if you have endometriomas (cysts), are considering surgery, or are on other medications. NAC interacts with nitrates and some blood thinners.
Where this evidence comes from
Good evidence
An Italian trial (Porpora et al., 2013) assigned 92 women with confirmed endometriosis cysts to either NAC or dienogest (a strong hormonal treatment for endo). After 3 months, the NAC group showed meaningful reductions in cyst size and reported pain improvement — and 21 of 47 women cancelled planned surgery due to improvement. Dienogest showed greater cyst reduction but was associated with more side effects.
What this means — this is a single, relatively small RCT. The results are striking but need replication before NAC can be considered standard of care. Nonetheless, the effect size, low cost and excellent safety profile make it a reasonable addition to a self-managed treatment strategy while awaiting further research or medical review.

Worth knowing

NAC is generally very well tolerated. The most common side effects are gastrointestinal (nausea, diarrhoea) — taking it with food usually helps. Because this is a single trial, we've graded it "Good evidence" rather than "Strong" — but the result is compelling enough that many women choose to try it while awaiting specialist review, particularly given the very low cost and risk.
