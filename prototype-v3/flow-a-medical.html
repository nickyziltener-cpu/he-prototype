Medication & medical treatment

5 options, ranked by evidence strength for IBS.

All options here require a prescription. We show you the evidence so you can have an informed conversation with your GP — not as a substitute for one.
Linaclotide
Strong evidence
A secretagogue that stimulates fluid secretion and accelerates gut motility. Licensed specifically for constipation-predominant IBS (IBS-C). Large RCTs show significant reduction in abdominal pain and constipation severity.
IBS-C only
Antispasmodics (mebeverine, hyoscine)
Good evidence
Relax smooth muscle in the gut to relieve cramping and abdominal pain. Mebeverine and hyoscine butylbromide are the most commonly prescribed first-line IBS medications — particularly effective for managing acute flares.
Low-dose amitriptyline
Good evidence
A tricyclic antidepressant used at low doses (10–30mg) to reduce pain signalling from the gut and slow intestinal transit. Multiple RCTs show meaningful improvement in pain and stool frequency — particularly effective for IBS-D.
Rifaximin
Moderate evidence
A non-absorbable antibiotic that targets gut bacteria without systemic absorption. A 2011 RCT (n=1,260) showed significant improvement in bloating and overall IBS symptoms for non-constipated IBS. More widely available in the US than the UK.
Low-dose SSRIs
Mixed evidence
At low doses, certain SSRIs (fluoxetine, paroxetine) may accelerate gut transit and improve overall wellbeing — particularly when anxiety or depression is a significant co-factor. Evidence is inconsistent across individual trials.
