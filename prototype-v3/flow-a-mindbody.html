Mind & Body

10 options, ranked by how strong the research is for IBS.

Gut-directed hypnotherapy
Strong evidence
Guided relaxation that retrains the gut–brain connection. Around 70–80% of patients report meaningful symptom improvement — and results hold at 12-month follow-up.
Learn more
Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
Strong evidence
Structured therapy targeting the thoughts and behaviours that amplify IBS symptoms. Several large RCTs show significant benefit, particularly for pain catastrophising and quality of life.
Mindfulness-based stress reduction
Good evidence
An 8-week programme training attention and non-reactivity to gut sensations. Particularly effective when stress or anxiety is a clear trigger for symptom flares.
Acceptance & commitment therapy (ACT)
Moderate evidence
Focuses on changing your relationship with symptoms rather than eliminating them — helps reduce the distress and avoidance that often amplify IBS. Growing evidence base, particularly for quality of life.
Exercise therapy
Moderate evidence
Regular moderate exercise — walking, swimming, cycling — reduces overall IBS symptom severity and improves quality of life in RCTs. Also addresses the fatigue and low mood that often accompany IBS.
Diaphragmatic breathing
Moderate evidence
Slow, deep belly breathing activates the parasympathetic nervous system and can reduce gut hypersensitivity. Simple to learn and can be practised anywhere — often used alongside other therapies.
Yoga & gentle movement
Moderate evidence
Several RCTs show improvement in overall IBS symptoms, particularly bloating and anxiety. The combination of movement, breathwork, and stress reduction makes it hard to attribute effects to a single mechanism.
Biofeedback
Limited evidence
Uses sensors to help you learn to consciously control gut and pelvic floor muscle activity. Most evidence is for IBS-C with outlet obstruction. Access is limited and sessions can be costly.
Progressive muscle relaxation
Limited evidence
Systematically tensing and releasing muscle groups to reduce whole-body stress response. Small studies show symptom improvement, often used as part of a broader stress-management programme.
Psychodynamic therapy
Limited evidence
Explores unconscious emotional conflicts that may be expressed through gut symptoms. Some RCT evidence exists but studies are small. Typically a longer-term commitment than CBT or hypnotherapy.
