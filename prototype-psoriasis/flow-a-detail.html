Emollients & moisturising

Daily moisturising is one of the most evidence-backed steps for psoriasis management. Consistent use reduces scaling, relieves itch, and significantly lowers the amount of prescription topical treatment needed. It's the foundation that makes everything else work better.

What it is

Emollients are moisturising treatments applied directly to the skin — creams, ointments, lotions, or bath additives. They work by forming a barrier on the skin surface, reducing water loss and softening the thick, scaly plaques that are characteristic of psoriasis. The more occluded the formulation (ointments > creams > lotions), the more effective — though patient preference matters for adherence.

How research suggests using it
1
Apply at least twice daily
Morning and evening as a minimum. Apply to the whole body, not just affected areas — healthy skin needs protection too. Use generous amounts.
2
Apply to damp skin
Apply immediately after bathing — within 3 minutes — while skin is still slightly damp. This locks in moisture rather than just sitting on top of dry skin.
3
Apply before prescription treatments
If using topical steroids or vitamin D analogues, applying emollient first — and letting it absorb for 30 minutes — improves penetration of the active treatments. Don't mix them together.
4
Choose based on your skin
Ointments (e.g. Diprobase, Epaderm) are most effective but can feel greasy. Creams suit daytime use. Match the product to the body site and your lifestyle — the best emollient is the one you'll actually use.
Where this evidence comes from
Strong evidence
A 2011 Cochrane review and multiple RCTs confirm that consistent emollient use significantly reduces plaque thickness, scaling, and the quantity of prescription treatments required. The NHS Psoriasis clinical guidelines and NICE recommend emollients as the first-line foundation for all severities.
What this means — the evidence here isn't a single trial, it's a consensus across guidelines, systematic reviews, and clinical practice. Moisturising is graded Strong not because of dramatic effect sizes, but because the effect is consistent, universal, and has essentially no downside.

What to look for on the label
Good options (ointments)
Epaderm · Diprobase ointment · 50:50 white soft paraffin/liquid paraffin · Cetraben
Good options (creams)
Doublebase · E45 · Diprobase cream · Aveeno · QV cream
What to avoid
Fragranced products · Alcohol-containing creams · Products with known irritants (preservatives like MI/MCI) if you have sensitive skin
Your pharmacist can recommend on-prescription emollients that can be dispensed at a lower cost than over-the-counter equivalents.
Worth knowing

Emollients are available on prescription — which means your GP can provide them significantly cheaper than buying them over the counter. If you're using emollients regularly (and you should be), it's worth asking for an emollient prescription at your next appointment. NHS patients pay the same flat prescription charge regardless of the product size.
